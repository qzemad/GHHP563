Behaviour, death and disease

Chapter 1 · pp. 9–12

  • Life expectancy rose enormously in the 20th century: in the UK from 47 years in 1900 to 81 in 2013
  • Much of the fall in deaths came from better living conditions, diet, hygiene and education, not only medicine
  • Today's leading killers (heart disease, stroke, cancer, lung disease) all have a behavioural component: smoking, alcohol, inactivity, poor diet

Key study

Peto and Lopez (1990) estimated that up to three-quarters of cancer deaths are attributable, at least in part, to our behaviour.

Murray and Lopez (1997) projected that heart disease, stroke, COPD, respiratory infections and lung cancers would be the top five killers by 2020.

Why it matters: if behaviour drives the top killers, then psychology, which studies behaviour, has a job in medicine.

Lay theories of health

Chapter 1 · pp. 12–15

  • Ordinary people define health in several ways at once: not being ill, having a reserve of health, being fit, feeling well, or being able to do things
  • Bennett (2000): health as being, having and doing
  • Health is judged by comparison, and the question you ask changes the answer
  • WHO (1947): "complete physical, mental and social well-being". Bircher (2005): well-being that meets the demands of life for your age and culture

Key study

Bauman (1961) asked people with serious illness what health meant. Three answers: a general feeling of well-being, the absence of symptoms, and what a fit person can do.

Nearly half gave two types of answer; 12% gave all three.

Health and Lifestyle survey:

  • Health as not ill
  • Health as a reserve
  • Health as behavior
  • Health as physical fitness and vitality
  • Health as Psychosocial well-being
  • Health as function

Why it matters: if "healthy" means "not ill", you won't see a doctor about high blood pressure, which usually causes no symptoms at all.

Cross-cultural perspectives on health

Chapter 1 · pp. 15–17

  • What counts as "normal" health, and its causes and cures, varies across cultures and eras
  • The West tends to split mind, body and soul; many cultures see health as whole and in balance (Chalmers 1996)
  • Collectivist cultures treat health as a family and community matter; individualist ones as a personal one
  • Many people use a healer and a clinic at the same time

Key study

Jobanputra and Furnham (2005) compared British Gujarati Indians with white British people. The Gujarati Indian group more often blamed illness on supernatural causes such as the evil eye.

Beliefs like these shape whether, when and where people seek help.

Lifespan, ageing and beliefs about health and illness

Chapter 1 · pp. 17–24

  • Children's ideas about illness develop in stages, in step with their thinking (Piaget)
  • Adolescents start risk behaviours as peers begin to outweigh parents
  • Ageing isn't the same as illness, but expecting poor health leads older people to skip check-ups and stop exercising (Sarkisian et al. 2001)
  • Older people judge their health differently: by how they measure up to others their age (Sargent-Cox et al. 2010)

Key study

Bibace and Walsh (1980) asked children aged 3–13 about illness:

  • Under 7: illness is its symptoms, caught almost magically ("a cold is when you sniff a lot")
  • About 8–11: germs get inside the body
  • Adolescence: body processes, then mind and body together

What is health psychology?

Chapter 1 · pp. 24–29

Psychology's contribution to promoting and maintaining health, preventing and treating illness, understanding its causes, and improving health care and policy (Matarazzo 1980).

Like all psychology it aims to describe, explain, predict and intervene, using the biopsychosocial model you met last week (Engel 1977).

Its neighbours

  • Psychosomatic medicine: personality and illness
  • Behavioural medicine: learning principles in treatment
  • Medical psychology: a profession, varies by country
  • Medical sociology: family, class and culture
  • Clinical psychology: mental health problems

Health differentials

Chapter 2 · Evidence of health differentials, p. 32 · Even the "haves" experience health differentials, pp. 33–34

  • Poverty is the biggest cause of ill health in the world
  • Life expectancy ranges from 86 in Japan to under 50 in Sierra Leone (WHO 2013)
  • In poorer countries a third of deaths happen before age 5; in rich ones two-thirds happen after 65
  • Even within rich countries, health falls with every step down the social ladder: a gradient, not a cliff

Key study

Marmot et al. (1991), the Whitehall studies of UK civil servants. Middle-class executives with one car were more likely to die earlier than those with two.

Nobody here is poor. Small differences in wealth still show up in health.

Pakistan: children in the poorest fifth of homes are about three times as likely to die before age 5 as those in the richest fifth (115 vs 38 per 1,000).

Social causation versus social drift

Chapter 2 · Explanations of socio-economic health inequalities · pp. 34–35

Social causation

Something about being low in society causes ill health. Poverty → illness

Social drift

Illness costs you work and income, so you drift down. Illness → poverty

Key study

Marmot et al. (1991) followed thousands of healthy people for years. Socio-economic status at the start predicted later health far better than health predicted later status.

Verdict: mostly causation.

Different health behaviours · Access to health care

Chapter 2 · pp. 35–38

  • Behaviour: poorer groups smoke and drink more, eat less healthily and exercise less, but not from ignorance. Smoking and cheap food are often ways of coping (Graham 1994)
  • Access: the poor use services, but get less care for their level of need. In Pakistan, about half of health spending comes straight from people's pockets
  • Also in this part: Environmental factors (p. 36): damp, crowded housing, pollution, dangerous work. Inequalities of health provision (p. 38): care differs by where you live

Key study

Nandi et al. (2014) followed over 8,000 older US adults. The poorest were nearly 3× as likely to die within ten years.

Behaviour explained about two-thirds of the gap. The other third came from low status itself.

The stress hypothesis

Chapter 2 · pp. 37–39

  • Life lower down brings more stress (insecure work, debt, crowding, little control) and fewer resources to cope
  • Hierarchy–health hypothesis: simply knowing you're low in the pecking order is stressful
  • Where you think you stand predicts health better than your actual income (Singh-Manoux et al. 2005)
  • Unequal societies have less social capital: trust, safety and belonging

Key study

Wilkinson (1990) compared nine Western countries. Their overall wealth didn't predict life expectancy. The gap between rich and poor did:

r = 0.86

Work status and stress · Work–life balance and stress · Unemployment

Chapter 2 · pp. 40–43

Job strain

Karasek: high demands plus little control (and little support) is the risk, not the busy boss. Also effort without reward (Siegrist et al. 1990).

Work–life balance

Stress at home adds to stress at work. Work–home spillover, mostly carried by women, is linked to anxiety, fatigue and poor sleep.

Unemployment

A job is better than no job. The harm of job loss comes mostly from financial insecurity (Ferrie et al. 2001).

Key studies: in over 10,000 UK civil servants, high demands with low control predicted heart disease (Kuper and Marmot 2003). People aged 51–61 who lost their jobs involuntarily had more heart attacks and strokes (Gallo et al. 2006).

Minority status and health

Chapter 2 · pp. 43–46 · Differential health behaviours · Stress · Accessing health care

  • "Ethnicity" bundles language, religion, migration, culture and identity: don't treat all minorities as one group (Nazroo 1998)
  • Much of the gap is class: allow for income and it shrinks
  • Behaviours differ between groups (diet, alcohol, exercise)
  • Stress from discrimination and harassment
  • Access: worse care even with the same illness; barriers like language and no female doctors

Key study

Clarke (2000) studied young African American women. The more racism they had experienced, the more their blood pressure rose during a stressful task.

Long-term prejudice may tune the body to react more strongly to stress.

Gender and health

Chapter 2 · pp. 46–48 · Biological differences · Behavioural differences · Economic and social factors

  • In most countries women live longer, largely because men get heart disease earlier. Yet men rate their health better and see doctors less
  • Biology: partly (women resist infection better), but hormone explanations don't hold up well
  • Behaviour: men drink and smoke more and seek help less
  • Economic and social: women are more often poorer and have less power over sex and contraception. Where maternal care is poor, the gap narrows. In Pakistan women live about 5 years longer

Key study

Mahalik et al. (2007): beliefs about masculinity predicted risky health behaviours, such as smoking and heavy drinking, better than education or income did.

"I haven't seen a doctor in years" is also a statement about being a man (Courtenay 2000).
