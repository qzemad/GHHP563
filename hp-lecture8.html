Section 1 of 2 · Morrison Chapter 9 · pp. 238–272

Symptom perception, interpretation and response

How people notice a change in the body, make sense of it, and decide what to do.

Why it matters: these three steps decide whether someone gets help in time.

Illness or disease? Three stages of response

Chapter 9 · How do we become aware of the sensations of illness? · pp. 240–242

Which of these have you had in the last two weeks? Which did you see a doctor about?

fever · nausea · headache · tremor · joint stiffness · excessive tiredness · back pain · dizziness · stomach pains · problems with your eyesight · chesty cough · sore throat · breathlessness · chest pain

A bodily sign can be measured, like a raised temperature. A symptom has to be interpreted: is that heat flu, or did you just run for the bus?

Illness is "what the patient feels when he goes to the doctor"; disease is "what he has on the way home" (Cassell 1976). You can feel ill with no disease, or have a disease and feel fine.

Between 70% and 90% of us will have a condition a doctor could treat, but only about a third will seek help. American students reported an average of 17 symptoms a month (Pennebaker and Skelton 1981).

Symptom perception: attention and individual differences

Chapter 9 · Symptom perception · pp. 242–248

Have you ever noticed an injury only after a cricket match ended? Why didn't you feel it during the match?

Competition of cues (Pennebaker 1982): our attention is limited, so signals from inside the body compete with what's happening outside. When the outside world is absorbing, we miss bodily signals; when it's boring, we notice them. That's why people cough more near the end of a lecture.

A sign is more likely to become a symptom if it is painful or disruptive, new, or long-lasting, or if the person already has a chronic illness. Too much attention also misleads: in "medical students' disease", over two-thirds of medical students reported the symptoms they were studying (Mechanic 1962).

Who notices what: men with prostate symptoms ignored them so as to look strong (Hale et al. 2007). Because people see heart disease as a "male" illness, women less often recognise their own heart attack (Martin et al. 2003). Negative mood and neuroticism increase the symptoms people report.

Coping style: monitors look for information about threats, and blunters avoid it (Miller et al. 1987). Which one would delay longer with chest pain?

Symptom interpretation: the common-sense model

Chapter 9 · Symptom interpretation · pp. 248–258 · Figure 9.4 redrawn

When you have a runny nose and aching joints, how do you know it's flu? What do you expect will happen next?

Disease prototypes: we compare symptoms with illness "templates" in memory. People delayed longest when their heart-attack symptoms were milder than their picture of a heart attack (Perry et al. 2001).

Leventhal's five dimensions of an illness representation: identity (its label and symptoms), cause, timeline (how long it will last), consequences, and cure or control.

In pairs: fill in the five dimensions for flu, then for type 2 diabetes. How do they differ?

The lay referral system: from "Dr Google" to ChatGPT

Chapter 9 · Culture and causal attributions · Planning and taking action · pp. 248–249, 261–264 · Not in the book: AI chatbots, self-medication

When you feel ill, who do you ask before a doctor: your mother, a friend, the pharmacist, or ChatGPT?

Illness behaviour is what people do before a diagnosis: rest, take medicine, ask others (Kasl and Cobb 1966). Sick role behaviour comes after a diagnosis (Parsons 1951). The lay referral system is the family, friends and colleagues who give advice first. Morrison says "Dr Google" has joined it.

Culture shapes the answer. Where traditional and modern medicine exist side by side, people use both, or go to doctors only when traditional remedies fail (Chalmers 1996). Malaysian Muslims believed more than Buddhists or Catholics that recovery depended on fate (Swami et al. 2009).

ChatGPT is the new Google (Not in the book): in a survey of 8,000 US adults in December 2025, 32% had used an AI chatbot for health information, double the figure for 2024, and 23% had used ChatGPT. Only 40% then checked with a doctor or other professional (Rock Health 2025).

Pakistan (Not in the book): in five Pakistani studies from the COVID period, between 58% and 86% of people said they self-medicated, mostly with painkillers and antibiotics bought without a prescription (Zheng et al. 2023).

Discuss: is a chatbot part of the lay referral system? How is asking ChatGPT different from asking your mother?

Delay behaviour: Safer's three-stage model

Chapter 9 · Planning and taking action: responding to symptoms · pp. 264–269 · Not in the book: Khan et al. (2007)

Over 40% of people who believed they were having a heart attack still waited more than an hour to call for help (Kentsch et al. 2002). Why?

Seekers believe the symptom is serious, find their life disrupted, or are pushed by someone they love. Delayers think "it's just a bug" or "I have no time", don't want to be a nuisance, or fear looking weak or hearing bad news (Table 9.2).

Karachi (Not in the book): the median time from heart-attack symptoms to reaching hospital was about 3 hours, and 34% took 6 hours or more (Khan et al. 2007). Embarrassing symptoms cause the longest appraisal delays.

Section 2 of 2 · Morrison Chapter 11 · pp. 302–336

Stress, health and illness: theory

Stress can be studied as an event, as the way a person sees the event, or as the body's response.

Why it matters: each view explains a different part of how stress could make Rashid Uncle ill.

Stress as a stimulus: life events and daily hassles

Chapter 11 · Concepts of stress · Stress as a stimulus · pp. 304–308 · Table 11.1

Read out the three things that stressed you this month. Are they big life events, or small everyday things?

Life event (Holmes and Rahe 1967)Units
Death of a spouse100
Divorce75
Marriage50
Being fired from work47
Change in where you live20

Life events theory: life changes add up, whether they are good or bad, and the more change people have had in a year, the more likely they are to fall ill. On the social readjustment rating scale, over 300 units in a year counts as a "major life crisis".

The limits: many studies asked people who were already ill to remember past events, and studies that followed people forward found much weaker links. Many items don't fit students, and the scale assumes everyone sees an event the same way.

Daily hassles (Kanner et al. 1981), like losing things, money worries and arguments, predict health better than major life events. Uplifts, like a compliment or a good grade, may protect us.

Stress as a transaction: Lazarus's cognitive appraisal model

Chapter 11 · Stress as a transaction · pp. 308–312 · Figure 11.1 redrawn

Two students sit the same exam tomorrow. One can't sleep; the other sleeps well. What's different?

Speisman et al. (1964): students watched the same disturbing film. Those given a detached commentary were less stressed than those who heard the pain emphasised.

Criticisms: the model is circular, because demands and coping are each defined by the other (Hobfoll 1989), and it isn't clear that both appraisals are needed (Zohar and Dayan 1999).

In pairs: is "I'll fail and lose my scholarship" harm, threat, challenge or benign?

Types of stress: acute and chronic

Chapter 11 · Types of stress · pp. 316–322 · Not in the book: the 2022 floods

Which is worse for your health: one terrible day, or a year of small, constant pressure?

Conservation of resources (Hobfoll 1989): stress follows the loss, or threatened loss, of things we value, such as a home, money, time or self-esteem. The more we lose, the harder it is to recover.

Acute stress: disasters. Survivors can show panic, survivor guilt, sleep problems and PTSD. Pakistan's 2022 floods affected more than 33 million people, about one in seven Pakistanis, and displaced 8 million (UN 2023, Not in the book).

Optional video · skip if short▶ After the floods · 3 min

Acute stress: exams. The Yerkes–Dodson law says a moderate level of arousal gives the best performance; too little or too much makes it worse. Students also get more infections around exam time.

Chronic stress: work. In Karasek's job demand–control model, high demands with low control cause the most strain. Burnout (Maslach) is emotional exhaustion, a cold, detached attitude to clients and a sense of achieving little; it is common in nurses and doctors.

Greenwich Works: for the night-shift workers project, what demands do night-shift workers face, and how much control do they have?

Stress as a physiological response: fight or flight and the GAS

Chapter 11 · Stress as a physiological response · pp. 322–326

Last week we saw the body's two stress routes. Who can remember what cortisol does?

Cannon (1932): adrenaline prepares the body to fight or flee. This helps in the moment, but it is harmful if it goes on.

Selye's general adaptation syndrome (1956): many different stressors cause the same response, in three stages. If the resistance stage lasts too long, the body's resources run out and "diseases of adaptation" such as heart disease become more likely.

Two systems: in the fast SAM system, the adrenal medulla releases adrenaline. In the slower HPA axis, the hypothalamus releases CRF, the pituitary releases ACTH, and the adrenal cortex releases cortisol, which peaks 20–40 minutes later.

Allostatic load (McEwen 2008): repeated stress causes "wear and tear". Later research found that different stressors cause different responses, so Selye's single response is too simple.

The stress and illness link

Chapter 11 · Stress and immune function · The stress and illness link · pp. 326–336

Can stress give you a cold? Hands up for yes, then for no.

Immunity: before exams, students' natural killer cells were less active (Kiecolt-Glaser et al. 1984), and a small wound took three days longer to heal (Marucha et al. 1998).

The common cold: volunteers were given cold viruses in nose drops. The more stressed they were, the more likely they were to catch a cold: a dose–response link (Cohen et al. 1993).

Heart disease: in 52 countries, stress at work, at home and about money was linked to heart attack (Rosengren et al. 2004). For cancer, there is no clear link.

Sapolsky's warning (1994): not everything bad in human health is caused by stress, "and shame on those who sell this view".
