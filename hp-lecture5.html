Why do we do what we do, and how do we change it?

Meet Bilal. 21, a student in Karachi. Smokes about 10 a day, mostly with friends after class and with chai. Never in front of his father. "I'll quit before I'm 30."

Section 1 of 4 · Chapter 5

Explaining behaviour

What makes people do, or not do, healthy things: their background, their beliefs, and the two most-used models.

Why it matters: you can only change a behaviour once you know what drives it. This section is the biggest part of the exam.

Background, or beliefs? Distal influences

Chapter 5 · Distal influences on health behaviour · pp. 124–125

Click once for distal, twice for proximal.

Why it matters: distal factors usually work through beliefs. We can't change Bilal's age or class, but we can change his beliefs, so models and interventions focus on proximal influences.

Social influences and Self-efficacy

Chapter 5 · Social influences; self-efficacy · pp. 128–129, 133–134 · Research focus: Stok et al. (2014)

Vote: which message would make you eat more fruit?

(a) the one that won: what others do

Descriptive norm = what others do. Injunctive norm = what others think you should do. Dutch teenagers given (a) ate 2.3 pieces of fruit a day vs 1.5 with (b) and 1.7 with no message (Stok et al. 2014). Being told "should" backfired.

Think of something hard you learned to do. What gave you the confidence?

Self-efficacy (Bandura)

Belief that "I can do it". Built by mastery (small successes) and modelling (seeing someone like you succeed). One of the most consistent predictors of behaviour, and part of nearly every model today.

For Bilal: he thinks "everyone smokes" (a descriptive norm, often an overestimate), and doubts he could quit (low self-efficacy).

The Health Belief Model

Chapter 5 · The health belief model · pp. 134–135 · Rosenstock (1974); Becker (1974)

Sort Bilal's thoughts: click 1× susceptibility · 2× severity · 3× benefit · 4× barrier

Health Belief Model: evidence and limitations

Chapter 5 · The health belief model: evidence and limitations · pp. 135–137

Where would the HBM fail to explain Bilal?

Where it works

Best for starting a protective behaviour (screening, vaccination, self-examination). Benefits and barriers predict most consistently. Women who did breast self-examination regularly had higher health motivation (Champion and Miller 1992).

Limitations

  • No rule for how the beliefs combine
  • Relies on threat; fear can backfire
  • Ignores friends and family
  • Weak for habits and addictions: past behaviour predicts better (Abraham et al. 1996)

Exam tip: "Discuss the HBM" = describe the components, apply them to the case, give one study, and name one limitation with a reason.

Theory of Planned Behaviour: where does an intention come from?

Chapter 5 · The theory of planned behaviour · pp. 137–139 · Ajzen (1985, 1991)

How well does it work? The TPB explains 35–50% of the differences between people in intention, but only 26–35% in behaviour (Sutton 2004). Intentions are easier to predict than actions.

Intention–behaviour gap

Chapter 5 · The intention–behaviour gap; implementation intentions · pp. 139, 143–147 · Gollwitzer (1993, 1999)

Hands up if you've ever intended to exercise, and didn't.

The intention–behaviour gap

Many people intend but don't act. Women's attitude, norms and PBC predicted intending to go for screening, but not all of them predicted actually going (Rutter 2000).

Implementation intentions: an "if–then" plan

Link the action to a specific cue: when, where, how. "IF it's 7 a.m. on a weekday, THEN I walk round the block." The cue then triggers the action automatically.

Guess: women who intended to check their breasts each month. How many did it a month later, with and without a written plan?

0

with a plan vs 14% without (Orbell et al. 1997)

Your turn (2 minutes): write one "IF…, THEN…" plan for something you keep putting off.

Exam-style question · 5 marksPractice for the long questions

Discuss the Theory of Planned Behaviour. Use it to explain why many university students in Karachi do not exercise regularly.

Write a plan: one or two lines for each part below. 8 minutes, on your own.

Describe · 1

The behaviour and the main idea: intention is the best predictor of behaviour.

Theory · 1

Ajzen's TPB: attitude, subjective norm, perceived behavioural control → intention → behaviour.

Explain · 2

Apply each part: "exercise is boring" (attitude); friends don't exercise (norm); heat, safety, no facilities, timetable (PBC, also the direct path).

Research · 1

One study with its finding, e.g. Sutton (2004): 26–35% of behaviour explained; or Hagger et al. (2002) on exercise.

The same four parts work for every "Discuss" question: the HBM, stage models, motivational interviewing, problem-solving.

Section 2 of 4 · Chapter 5

Stages of change

Models that see change as a series of steps, each needing different help.

Why it matters: help that suits one stage can backfire at another. Telling someone who isn't ready how to quit doesn't work.

Stages of change: Transtheoretical Model

Chapter 5 · The transtheoretical model · pp. 149–150 · Prochaska and DiClemente (1984)

"Smoking's fine. Why would I stop?"
"I should quit in the next few months. Not quite yet."
"My quit date is the 1st. I've told my mum."
"I stopped three weeks ago."
"Eight months without a cigarette."

Stage models: matching help, criticisms, PAPM and HAPA

Chapter 5 · Stage models of behaviour change · pp. 150–155

Bilal isn't ready to quit. Would a leaflet on "10 ways to stop smoking" help him? What would?

Precontemplation: raise awareness; motivational interviewing

Contemplation: weigh pros and cons; resolve doubts

Preparation: set a date; make if–then plans

Action: cope with cravings; support

Maintenance: avoid triggers; prevent relapse

Criticisms: The time cut-offs are arbitrary, and the stages may just be points on one continuum (Kraft et al. 1999). Many people don't fit one stage. A review of 37 trials found only limited support for stage-matched help (Bridle et al. 2005).

Section 3 of 4 · Chapter 6

Motivating change

Getting people to want to change: planning a programme, persuading, fear, framing and motivational interviewing.

Why it matters: the UK's frightening AIDS adverts of the 1980s changed nobody's condom use. "Health education doesn't work!"

Planning a programme: PRECEDE–PROCEED

Chapter 6 · Developing public health interventions: PRECEDE–PROCEED · pp. 160–161 · Green and Kreuter (2005)

Getting Karachi students to exercise: what's needed? Click 1× predisposing · 2× enabling · 3× reinforcing

Two routes to persuasion: ELM

Chapter 6 · Motivating change: the elaboration likelihood model · pp. 162–164 · Petty and Cacioppo (1986)

Which advert would make you quit? Vote.

Why it matters: match the message to the audience's interest, and pre-test it with a small group first. Information alone works only if it is new, relevant, easy to act on and doesn't clash with what people already believe.

Fear appeals: the Extended Parallel Process Model

Chapter 6 · The use of fear · p. 164 · Chapter 7 · p. 186 · Witte (1992)

Before we watch: an Australian AIDS advert from 1987. Write down how it makes you feel.

Play "Grim Reaper" on YouTube ↗ about 1 minute

Would it make you use condoms? What would you do after watching?

What happened

This advert and the UK's "gravestone" advert raised anxiety, but changed neither knowledge nor behaviour (Rigby et al. 1989; Sherr 1987).

The rule: some fear, plus a clear action that works and that people can do. Australian beach skin-cancer warnings came with free sunscreen.

Exam-style question · 2.5 marksChapter 6 · Information framing · p. 164

Gain or loss?

A gain-framed message stresses what you gain by acting. A loss-framed message stresses what you lose by not acting. Neither is reliably better, so pilot-test.

Karachi's dengue season peaks after the monsoon. Write one gain-framed and one loss-framed message to persuade families to remove standing water around their homes. Explain which you would choose and why.

Write your answer on paper. 5 minutes, on your own.

Gain-framed

"Empty your coolers and pots every week, and keep your family safe from dengue."

Loss-framed

"Leave water standing, and you could spend a week in hospital with a child with dengue."

A full answer also says why, and adds an easy action (EPPM): "cover tanks, empty pots every Sunday". Preventive actions are often argued to suit gain frames; detection (screening) loss frames, but the evidence is mixed.

Section 4 of 4 · Chapter 6

Helping people change

Once someone wants to change: helping them work out how.

Why it matters: about 70% of smokers try to quit each year. Wanting to isn't the problem; doing it is.

Problem-solving counselling: the case of Mrs T

Chapter 6 · Problem-solving approaches; case history · pp. 166–167 · Egan

Mrs T is overweight with high cholesterol. For months her doctor gave her leaflets and recipes. She knows what's healthy. Nothing changed.

Why isn't she losing weight? What would you ask her?

1

Her sons came home from the pub late, wanting fry-ups. She nibbled while cooking.

2

Then she thought "I've blown it", and ate a full meal.

3

Her own goal: stop cooking for them late at night. She rehearsed telling them, and lost weight.

The counsellor guides; the person finds the answers. Factory workers given this kind of counselling had bigger falls in blood pressure, weight and smoking than those given health education (Gomel et al. 1993).

Modelling, practice and cognitive interventions

Chapter 6 · Modelling and practice; cognitive interventions · pp. 169–171 · Bandura (2001); Beck (1976)

Modelling and practice

Watch someone do it: live, described, or on screen. A coping model, someone like you who finds it hard but manages, works best: "if he can, I can". Then practise it (role-play saying no) with feedback.

Challenging beliefs (cognitive)

Treat a belief as a guess to test, not a fact. Socratic questions: "I can't get through exams without smoking." "What happened in exams before you smoked?" Then a homework test: one exam week without cigarettes.

Students act out refusing a cigarette, and the teacher gives feedback.
