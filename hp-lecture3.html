What is health behaviour?

Chapter 3 · pp. 52–53

Kasl and Cobb, 1966

"Any activity undertaken by a person believing themselves to be healthy for the purposes of preventing disease or detecting it at an asymptomatic stage."

Harris and Guten, 1979

Behaviour performed regardless of perceived health status, to protect, promote or maintain health. So people who are already ill count too.

The catch

Both assume the goal is health. But people exercise to look good, to meet friends, or for fun.

The general definition

Health behaviour is any behaviour associated with a person's health status, whatever their current health or motives.

Health behaviour (feel well): a check-up, eating well

Illness behaviour (have symptoms): seeing a doctor about a cough

Sick-role behaviour (diagnosed): taking the medicine, resting

Matarazzo: behavioural pathogens and immunogens

Chapter 3 · p. 52

Behavioural pathogen

A behaviour thought to damage health, e.g. smoking. The book calls these health-risk behaviours.

Behavioural immunogen

A behaviour that protects health, e.g. exercise. The book calls these health-protective behaviours.

Matarazzo (1984)

Quick sort: click once for pathogen (red), twice for immunogen (green).

The Alameda 7

Chapter 3 · p. 53 · Belloc and Breslow (1972)

6,928 healthy adults in Alameda County, California, followed for over 30 years (1965–1999). Seven behaviours predicted who stayed healthy and lived longer:

  1. Sleeping 7–8 hours a night
  2. Not smoking
  3. No more than 1–2 alcoholic drinks a day
  4. Regular exercise
  5. Not eating between meals
  6. Eating breakfast
  7. No more than 10% overweight

The payoff

People doing 6 or 7 of these lived longer than those doing fewer than 6:

+7

years, men

+11

years, women

But: later re-analyses found snacking and skipping breakfast didn't predict death. The benefits multiply: two good habits gave more than twice the benefit of one.

The burden of ill health: DALYs

Chapter 3 · p. 55

Disability-adjusted life years

Years of healthy life lost: years lost to early death plus years lived with illness or disability. One number for mortality and morbidity.

DALY = years of life lost + years lived with disability

In the EU, tobacco and alcohol cause more lost healthy years than any other behaviour, far more than illicit drugs (Anderson and Baumberg 2006).

Quick check: which loses more DALYs?

A: dies of a heart attack at 50 (expected to live to 75)
B: lives 40 years with diabetes complications, weighted 0.3

Deaths alone would miss person B entirely.

0

Pakistan

About 59% of adults overweight and 24% obese on WHO cut-offs. Obesity has roughly tripled in 20 years.

On Asian cut-offs, a 2018 survey of 19,000+ Pakistanis found about half obese.

Why it matters: Pakistan has one of the highest diabetes rates in the world. Obesity is no longer a "rich country" problem.

Negative health consequences of obesity

Chapter 3 · p. 82

  • Body: high blood pressure, heart disease, type 2 diabetes, osteoarthritis, breathing problems, back pain, some cancers
  • Mind: low self-esteem and social isolation, often from stigma. Overweight children have poorer quality of life
  • High BMI caused an estimated 3.7 million deaths in 2021 (WHO)

Key study

Flegal et al. (2013): 97 studies, 2.88 million people. Compared with "normal" weight:

  • Obese grades 2–3: 29% higher risk of death
  • Grade 1 obese: no difference
  • Overweight: slightly lower risk (6%)

A J-shaped curve: underweight is risky too.

What causes obesity?

Chapter 3 · pp. 83–84

Energy in > energy out

The simple version (Pinel 2003). True, but not the whole story.

Genes

Adopted children's weight tracks their biological mothers. More fat cells? Little evidence. Slower metabolism? Not supported. Appetite hormones (leptin), FTO gene: partly.

Activity can cancel genes

Vigorous activity may cancel out genetic risk from the FTO gene (Rampersaud et al. 2008).

Sedentary life

Indoors, screens, cars. TV and computing may even lower metabolic rate.

Eating styles

External (sight and smell of food), internal (hunger), emotional (bored, stressed). Stress shifts us to sweet, fatty food.

An "obesogenic" environment

Surroundings that push overeating and inactivity. In Karachi: cheap fried street food and delivery apps; sugary drinks everywhere; heat, traffic and few parks or footpaths, so we drive.

Why do people initiate addictive substance use? (1)

Chapter 3 · pp. 64–65

Genetics

Some evidence involving dopamine reception, but genes don't act alone (Munafò and Johnstone 2008).

Curiosity

"What does it feel like?" The most common reason for a first cigarette, drink or joint (Morrison and Plant 1991).

Modelling and social learning

Parents, older siblings and friends who smoke make a child more "prepared" to start. Siblings may matter even more than peers (Mercken et al. 2007).

Social pressure?

15–16-year-olds rejected the idea of peer pressure: they said they chose to smoke (Denscombe 2001).

Image and reputation

Fitting in and status. Boys gained status through fitness; for girls, smoking could signal being cool, sophisticated or rebellious (Michell and Amos 1997). Low family cohesion strengthens peers' pull.

Why do people initiate addictive substance use? (2)

Chapter 3 · pp. 65–66

Self-concept and self-esteem

Girls who smoked had less self-confidence, felt less liked by their families and had lower physical and social self-concepts (Snow and Bruce 2003).

Weight control

A reason to start and keep smoking, more often among girls (French et al. 1994), though boys do it too.

Risk-taking propensity

Early smoking, drinking and cannabis cluster with truancy and petty theft. Sensation-seeking rises through adolescence (Collado et al. 2014).

Health cognitions

Expecting stress relief; unrealistic optimism: "I won't smoke as much as others, so it won't affect me".

Stress: key study

Byrne and Mazanov (2003) followed 2,600 Australian teenagers. Non-smokers who experienced stress over the next year were more likely to start. For girls, school stress, family conflict, parental control and feeling school was irrelevant all predicted starting.

Continuing unhealthy behaviour and developing dependency

Chapter 3 · pp. 66–68

The reasons people continue are not the same as the reasons they started. Only about 1 in 10 heavy drinkers becomes dependent, so dependence can't explain all the harm.

  • Genes and family history: children of problem drinkers are at higher risk
  • Personality and mood: anxiety, sensation-seeking, mood disorders
  • Psychological reasons: pleasure, habit, coping with stress, not believing you can stop (low self-efficacy)

The nicotine trap

  1. Nicotine triggers dopamine in the brain's reward pathways
  2. Tolerance: more is needed for the same effect
  3. Levels drop → withdrawal: cravings, irritability, anxiety, sleeplessness
  4. Smoking again removes the withdrawal: that relief is the reward
  5. A vicious cycle

Behaviour cessation and thoughts about treating dependence

Chapter 3 · pp. 68–72

Who stops?

Most smokers want to stop. Those with higher status and education quit more (Droomers et al. 2002). Not being in a smoking network helps more than health beliefs do (Rose et al. 1996). Fear of weight gain holds some back.

How we've seen dependence over time:

  • 17th–18th c. · Moral weakness → punishment
  • 19th c. · Victim of an evil substance → prohibition (USA)
  • 1935–1960 · A disease of the person (Jellinek 1960) → lifelong abstinence (Alcoholics Anonymous)
  • Behaviourism · Learned behaviour → unlearn it; controlled drinking, methadone

Two targets: primary prevention (stop people starting) and secondary prevention (help users change). Should we spend more on tobacco and alcohol than on illegal drugs?

Unhealthy diet: cancer and food

Chapter 3 · p. 78 · updated with IARC (2015) and recent rulings

0

of cancer deaths are partly due to poor diet: high fat, high salt, low fibre. Smoking: about 30% (American Cancer Society 2012).

Processed meat

IARC (WHO) 2015: processed meat causes bowel cancer; red meat probably does. 50 g a day raises risk by 18%: relative risk, about 1–2 extra cases per 100 people.

The food colours controversy

  • 2007: the Southampton study: mixes of six artificial colours increased hyperactivity in 3- and 8–9-year-olds (McCann et al.)
  • 2010: the EU requires a warning: "may have an adverse effect on activity and attention in children"
  • 2025: the US bans Red No. 3, which caused thyroid cancer in rats

Here: bright mithai, sherbets, jelly sweets, street chutneys. Who checks the colours?

Fat intake and cholesterol

Chapter 3 · pp. 78–79

LDL: "bad" cholesterol

Lays down fatty plaques in the arteries (atherosclerosis). Raised by saturated and trans fats.

HDL: "good" cholesterol

Helps the liver clear LDL. Raised by oily fish (omega-3) and exercise.

Aim: total cholesterol to HDL ratio below 4.5 : 1. Cutting blood cholesterol by 10% cut heart disease by 54% at age 40 (Law et al. 1994).

Sort the Pakistani plate: once = pushes LDL up (red), twice = better choice (green)

Newer understanding of fat

Chapter 3 · p. 80 (Issues box) · updated

Saturated fat: not the main villain?

A meta-analysis of 72 studies didn't clearly link saturated fat to heart disease, but trans fats were clearly harmful (Chowdhury et al. 2014).

Swapping fat for sugar doesn't help

In 135,000 people in 18 countries, including Pakistan, high-carbohydrate diets were linked to higher death rates (PURE study, Dehghan et al. 2017). Its conclusions are debated.

Ultra-processed food

A 2024 review linked ultra-processed food to 32 health outcomes, including heart disease, type 2 diabetes and depression (Lane et al., BMJ).

Pakistan, April 2025

Industrial trans fats are now limited to under 2% of total fat in all foods. Enforcement is the next challenge.

Today's message: cut trans fats and ultra-processed food; eat a balanced, Mediterranean-style diet.

0

Negative health effects: alcohol and drugs

Chapter 3 · pp. 61–64

  • Alcohol is a depressant, not a stimulant. Heavy intoxication makes accidents 25× more likely; very high doses can stop breathing
  • The more you drink over time, the more cirrhosis, liver and oesophageal cancer, stroke and epilepsy
  • 1 in 4 deaths of young men aged 15–29 in the EU are due to alcohol
  • Is moderate drinking protective? The "J-curve" may be an artefact: non-drinkers include people who quit because they were already ill

Drugs

How a drug is taken matters as much as what it is. About 12.7 million people inject drugs: 13% have HIV and more than half have hepatitis C.

The book's point: illegal drugs cause far less overall harm than tobacco and alcohol, yet attract far more fear.

Five things to take away

  • Health behaviour is anything linked to health status, whatever the motive: pathogens and immunogens
  • A handful of risk factors drives most cardiovascular death; DALYs count illness as well as death
  • Smoking is falling but still kills 448 Pakistanis a day; quitting at any age helps
  • Obesity is rising fastest in countries like Pakistan; genes, environment and eating style all play a part
  • People start for social and emotional reasons and continue for different ones, including dependence
