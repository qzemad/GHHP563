Health Psychology, Lecture 9 · Morrison and Bennett, Chapter 12

Stress and illness moderators

Why the same stress makes one person ill and leaves another well

Today ends with a group activity and a short quiz of two questions, so keep notes as we go.

Same stress, different outcome: what protects Tariq?

Running case (invented, from Lectures 6–8) · Chapter 12 introduction, p. 339

Rashid Uncle and his brother Tariq run shops side by side. They have the same market, the same load-shedding and the same money worries. Rashid had a heart attack, but Tariq is well. Give me three possible reasons.

A moderator is something that changes how strongly stress affects health. Morrison describes four kinds, and today has one section on each.

Section 1 of 4 · Morrison Chapter 12 · pp. 340–346

Coping

What you do about a stressor can matter as much as the stressor itself.

Coping defined: Lazarus and Folkman's transactional model

Chapter 12 · Coping defined · pp. 340–341 · Figure 12.1 redrawn

Your laptop crashes the night before a deadline. Write down the first two things you would do.

Coping is anything a person thinks or does to reduce the impact of a stressor. It is purposeful, it is learned, and it changes over time (Lazarus and Folkman 1984). It doesn't have to remove the stressor: tolerating it, re-thinking it or minimising it also count.

Five functions of coping (Cohen and Lazarus 1979): reducing harmful conditions, tolerating negative events, keeping a positive self-image, keeping emotional balance, and keeping good relationships with others.

Problem-focused and emotion-focused coping

Chapter 12 · Coping defined · pp. 341–342 · Table 12.1 · Not in the book: Salman et al. (2020)

Look at your two laptop answers. Which tries to fix the problem, and which tries to calm you down?

Problem-focused coping tries to change the stressor, or to increase your resources to deal with it. Examples are planning, asking for practical help and confronting the problem.

Emotion-focused coping tries to manage the feelings the stressor causes. Examples are positive reappraisal, acceptance, seeking emotional support, venting and praying.

Approach and avoidant coping is a second way to sort coping. Approach coping faces the stressor and seeks information; avoidant coping keeps it out of mind, for example through distraction. People use several kinds at once: after a heart attack, patients used both acceptance and active problem-solving (Lowe et al. 2000).

Pakistan (Not in the book): during the 2020 lockdown, Pakistani university students' most used coping strategy was religious or spiritual coping, followed by acceptance (Salman et al. 2020).

Coping styles and adaptive coping: monitoring, blunting and fit

Chapter 12 · Coping styles or strategies; What is adaptive coping? · pp. 342–346

Before results come out, do you check the portal every hour, or avoid thinking about it until the day comes?

Coping styles are habits a person uses in most situations. Coping strategies change with the situation.

Monitoring and blunting (Miller 1987): monitors look for information about a threat, and blunters distract themselves from it. In 63 cancer studies, monitors knew more but felt more at risk and more upset (Roussi and Miller 2014).

The stressor can be changedThe stressor can't be changed
Problem-focusedGood fit: revise for the examPoor fit: frustration
Emotion-focusedPoor fit if used aloneGood fit: after a death or a diagnosis

Adaptive coping depends on the "goodness of fit" between the coping and the situation, so no strategy works everywhere. Being able to switch strategies, called coping flexibility, helps most.

Section 2 of 4 · pp. 346–357

Personality

Some people are more vulnerable to stress, and some are protected, because of who they are.

The Big Five: neuroticism and conscientiousness

Chapter 12 · Stress, personality and illness · pp. 346–349 · Research Focus: Gartland et al. (2014)

Think of the calmest person you know and the biggest worrier. Do they get ill equally often?

The Big Five personality traits are openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness and neuroticism.

Neuroticism is a tendency to feel anxious, worried and moody. People high in it see more events as threats, report more symptoms, and use more emotion-focused and avoidant coping. Part of the link to illness is in the reporting of symptoms, not real disease.

Conscientiousness means being organised, self-disciplined and responsible. It predicts healthier behaviour and a longer life. In a daily diary study, conscientious people also reported fewer daily hassles (Gartland et al. 2014).

Protective resources: optimism and hardiness

Chapter 12 · Optimism; Hardiness and resilience · pp. 349–354 · Figure 12.3 redrawn

"Every cloud has a silver lining." Agree or disagree? Hands up.

Dispositional optimism (Scheier and Carver) is a stable expectation that good things will happen. Optimists keep trying, cope more actively, and are less likely to blame themselves in a permanent way. It is measured with the Life Orientation Test.

Hardiness (Kobasa 1979) has three Cs: commitment (a sense of purpose), control (believing you can influence events) and challenge (seeing change as a chance to grow).

Criticism: the buffering findings came mostly from cross-sectional studies, and prospective studies haven't always repeated them.

Optional video · skip if short▶ Hardiness · 1.5 min

Hardiness works as a buffer: it made the biggest difference when stress was high (Kobasa et al. 1982).

Type A behaviour, hostility, Type C and Type D personality

Chapter 12 · Type A; Hostility and anger; Type C; Type D · pp. 354–357

Picture someone who honks the moment the light turns green, eats lunch at the desk and interrupts everyone. Is that person heading for a heart attack?

Type A behaviour (Friedman and Rosenman 1959) means competitiveness, time urgency, impatience, hostility and fast, loud speech. Early studies found a small link to heart disease, but the 22-year follow-up and most later studies did not.

Hostility is the part of Type A most consistently linked to heart disease. Hostile people have bigger blood-pressure reactions to stress and fewer friends to buffer it.

Type C means holding emotions in, especially anger. It has been linked to cancer, but the evidence is weak.

Type D, the "distressed" type (Denollet 1998), combines negative affectivity with social inhibition. It has been linked to death after a heart attack, but later results are mixed.

Rashid Uncle shouts at customers and keeps his worries to himself. Which types fit him?

Section 3 of 4 · pp. 358–364

Beliefs and emotions

Whether you believe you can control events, and what you do with your feelings, both change how stress affects you.

Stress and cognitions: perceived control and self-efficacy

Chapter 12 · Stress and cognitions · pp. 358–361

Finish this sentence honestly: "Whether I stay healthy depends mostly on…"

Locus of control (Rotter 1966): people with an internal locus believe outcomes depend on their own actions. People with an external locus believe outcomes depend on luck, fate or powerful others. Internals use more problem-focused coping.

Multidimensional health locus of control (Wallston et al. 1978)Example item
Internal"I am in control of my health."
Chance"No matter what I do, if I am going to get sick, I will get sick."
Powerful others"Following my doctor's advice is the best way to stay healthy."

Self-efficacy is the belief that you can do what is needed. Students high in self-efficacy see exams as a challenge rather than a threat, in five countries (Luszczynska et al. 2005).

Discuss: is "it's in God's hands" an external locus of control, or can it sit alongside doing everything you can?

Stress and emotions: depression and emotional disclosure

Chapter 12 · Stress and emotions; Emotional disclosure · pp. 362–364

When something is really bothering you, do you talk about it, write it down, or keep it to yourself?

Depression and heart disease: in about 20,000 people without heart disease, major depression made death from heart disease 2.7 times more likely over about 8.5 years, even after other risks were controlled for (Surtees et al. 2008). After a heart attack, depression predicted death more strongly than the amount of heart damage (Frasure-Smith et al. 1995).

Emotional disclosure (Pennebaker): people who wrote about their feelings about a trauma for about 15 minutes on several days in a row had better immune function, faster wound healing and fewer doctor visits. A meta-analysis of 146 studies supports it (Frattaroli 2006).

Disclosure is not the same as venting. Repeatedly venting negative emotion can keep the feeling going and push support away.

Greenwich Works: for the student anxiety project, how could you test whether a short writing task lowers anxiety before exams?

Section 4 of 4 · pp. 364–370

Social support

The people around us are a resource that changes how stress affects us.

Video · 5 min · play now▶ WHO's Science in 5: Social isolation

Social support: types and functions

Chapter 12 · Definition, types and functions of social support · pp. 364–366 · Table 12.3 · Not in the book: WHO (2025)

Your father is in hospital. Name three people who would help, and say how each one would help.

Social support is the belief that you are loved, cared for, valued and part of a network of people who help each other.

Type (Table 12.3)What it looks like
EmotionalEmpathy, care and reassurance
EsteemEncouragement that builds your sense of worth
Tangible (instrumental)Money, practical help, a lift to the hospital
InformationalAdvice and suggestions
NetworkBelonging to a group with shared experiences

Perceived support, believing help is there, usually predicts health better than received support. Being isolated is not the same as feeling lonely.

WHO (2025, Not in the book): about one in six people worldwide is affected by loneliness, which is linked to about 100 deaths every hour.

Social support and health: direct effects and buffering

Chapter 12 · Social support and mortality; How does social support influence health status? · pp. 366–370

Do friends keep you healthy all the time, or only when things go wrong?

Alameda County study: people with the fewest social ties were almost twice as likely to die over the follow-up (Berkman and Syme 1979).

Meta-analysis of 148 studies: people with good social relationships had a 50% lower risk of death (Holt-Lunstad et al. 2010).

The buffering hypothesis (Cohen and Wills 1985) says support protects mainly under high stress, by changing appraisal ("I can cope with their help") or coping.

Culture: people in Asian, collectivist cultures often avoid "burdening" others by asking for help. For them, implicit support, knowing people are there without asking, lowered stress and cortisol (Taylor et al. 2007).

Group activity: the four cases

All cases are invented

A. Rashid Uncle, after his heart attack. He keeps his worries to himself and snaps at customers. He skips his cardiac check-ups, saying "What will happen will happen." His sons call him every day, but he says he doesn't want to be a burden.

B. Sana, a final-year student, three weeks before exams. She has made a revision timetable and joined a study group. She says "I've done this before; I can do it again," and prays before each paper. Her parents live abroad.

C. Bilal, 24, who works night shifts at a call centre. He has strict targets, angry callers and no say over his shifts. He sleeps through the day, rarely sees his family, and spends his free time scrolling alone.

D. Mrs Farooqi, 60, who cares for her husband with dementia. She does everything herself. Relatives give her advice but no practical help, and she says she "shouldn't burden anyone."
