Problems of neurological functioning: stroke

Chapter 8 · Problems of neurological functioning · pp. 210–211 · Not in the book: stroke awareness in Karachi

If your grandmother suddenly couldn't lift one arm and her speech slurred, what would you do, and how fast?

A stroke (cerebrovascular accident, CVA) happens when part of the brain loses its blood supply and the nerve cells there die. A TIA (transient ischaemic attack) is a temporary blockage, and its symptoms reverse.

After a stroke, people may be weak or paralysed on one side, struggle to speak or to understand speech, and have problems with memory, fatigue and emotions.

FFace: ask the person to smile. Does one side droop?
AArms: ask them to raise both arms. Does one drift down?
SSpeech: ask them to repeat a phrase. Is it slurred or strange?
TTime: if you see any of these, call for emergency help at once.

▶ NHS “Act FAST” · 1 min Clot-dissolving drugs only work if given quickly.

Karachi (Not in the book): in a survey of 398 adults at a large hospital, only half (50.8%) knew that a stroke affects the brain, and 29% thought it affects the heart (Aly et al. 2009).

The autonomic nervous system and endocrine processes

Chapter 8 · The autonomic nervous system; endocrine processes · pp. 211–214 · Table 8.1

Think of the moment just before an exam or a viva. What did your body do?

The autonomic nervous system adjusts the organs to match demand. It has two opposing arms: the sympathetic arm prepares us for "fight or flight", and the parasympathetic arm lets us "rest and recover".

Immune dysfunction: stress and HIV infection

Chapter 8 · Central nervous system links with the immune system; human immunodeficiency virus infection · pp. 215–217

Why do so many students catch a cold just after exams?

The book concludes that chronic stress "significantly impairs the effectiveness of the immune system" (p. 215).

HIV attacks the coordinator. The virus infects the helper T (CD4+) cells, so the other cells stop getting the signal to attack. When the count falls below 500, minor infections appear. Below 200, life-threatening infections and cancers occur, and the condition is called AIDS.

Treatment depends on behaviour. Combination drugs (ART) can suppress the virus to undetectable levels, and some people treated early may live as long as anyone else. The drugs can't remove the virus, so they must be taken strictly and for life.

Why a psychologist needs it: this is the biology behind "stress makes you ill", and helping people keep taking their medicine is one of health psychology's main jobs.

Autoimmune conditions

Chapter 8 · Autoimmune conditions: diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, multiple sclerosis · pp. 217–220 · Case histories: Mrs K and Ms F

What is the difference between type 1 and type 2 diabetes?

In an autoimmune condition, the immune system mistakes the body's own cells for invaders ("non-self") and attacks them.

Type 1 diabetes: the immune system destroys the cells in the pancreas that make insulin, often after a viral infection. People need insulin injections every day and must balance food against insulin.

Type 2 diabetes: the body makes insulin, but the cells stop responding to it. It develops slowly and is linked to obesity. The first treatment is weight loss and exercise, which many people find hard to keep up.

Mrs K, 42, has rheumatoid arthritis. Her immune system inflames the lining of her joints. "I use a knife with an oversized grip handle to make sandwiches… Exercise makes me feel good and gives me a sense of control over my body."

Ms F has multiple sclerosis. Her immune system attacks the coating of her nerves. "People don't like people in wheelchairs. You are ignored… and just want to say, 'Hey, I'm here. I have a brain you know.'"

Section 3 of 3 · Chapter 8 · pp. 221–234

The gut, the heart and the lungs

These are the digestive, cardiovascular and respiratory systems, where chronic disease shows up.

Why it matters: Pakistan's biggest killers develop here, and behaviour drives all three.

Disorders of the digestive system

Chapter 8 · Disorders of the digestive system: gastric ulcer, irritable bowel syndrome · pp. 221–224

Does stress cause stomach ulcers? Hands up for yes. Now hands up for no.

The gut has its own nervous system, and it is linked to the autonomic nervous system. Sympathetic activity slows digestion, which we feel as "butterflies in the stomach".

Gastric ulcer

Then: doctors believed stress raised stomach acid, and the acid caused the ulcer.

Now: a bacterium, Helicobacter pylori, is responsible for about 70% of cases. Stress may still play a part, because it increases smoking and drinking and weakens the immune system.

Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS)

Then: it has no visible physical cause, so it was called the "archetypal psychosomatic disorder".

Now: it may start with a gut infection and be kept going by anxiety and stress (Spence and Moss-Morris 2007). Cognitive-behavioural therapy is an effective treatment.

Also in the book: Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis are inflammatory bowel diseases that come in flare-ups and remissions. The early symptoms of colorectal cancer are mild, so people often seek help late.

The lesson: "it's all in the mind" and "it's all in the body" are both too simple.

The cardiovascular system: hypertension

Chapter 8 · Blood pressure; hypertension · pp. 228–229 · In the spotlight: John Henry · p. 231

Rashid Uncle has had high blood pressure for years and feels fine. If it has no symptoms, why bother treating it?

Blood pressure is written as two numbers: systolic (the surge as the heart pumps) over diastolic (the pressure between beats). In about 95% of cases there is no single disease behind high blood pressure. This is essential hypertension, and it rises slowly over years with obesity, lack of exercise and salt. It is "silent", but it damages the heart, brain, kidneys and eyes.

John Henryism (p. 231): John Henry raced a steam drill, won, and died of exhaustion. People who keep battling stressors such as poverty and racism may push their blood pressure up for good. It is now linked to low socio-economic position in general.

Coronary heart disease

Chapter 8 · Coronary heart disease: atherosclerosis, myocardial infarction, angina · pp. 230–232

Back to Rashid Uncle's chest pain. Is it a heart attack, angina, or gas? What would you need to know?

A heart attack classically brings "crushing" chest pain, pain down the left arm, breathlessness, sweating and nausea. The symptoms can also be vague, and many people mistake them for heartburn or indigestion and wait. "Clot-buster" drugs work best within about an hour.

Rashid Uncle: his pain came on at rest and hasn't eased, and he smokes and has high blood pressure. That is an emergency, and he shouldn't wait until morning.

Diseases of the respiratory system

Chapter 8 · Diseases of the respiratory system: chronic obstructive airways disease, lung cancer · pp. 232–234

Try this: pinch your nose shut, cover your mouth so that you can only just breathe through your fingers, and march on the spot for 30 seconds. Sit this out if you have asthma or any breathing problem.

"Now, do you see how hard it is to breathe? … That is what emphysema is." (Gary Bain, a patient, p. 234)

COPD (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease) is lasting damage to the air sacs and airways of the lungs. Emphysema brings shortness of breath and a dry cough. Chronic bronchitis brings a wet cough for three months or more, two years running. The main cause is smoking, including other people's smoke, and about 15% of long-term smokers develop COPD.

The psychology: breathlessness is frightening, so people stop exercising. Their lungs then get weaker, and they become breathless sooner. Pulmonary rehabilitation breaks the cycle with gentle exercise and coaching on coping with breathlessness.

Lung cancer is the second most common cancer in both sexes, and smoking is the main cause. Rates in women rose after women took up smoking.

Discuss: Karachi has smoking, vaping and polluted air. Which of last week's public health tools would protect lungs best?

Exam-style practice: Sana's two stressful years

A new scenario · 5 marks · uses pp. 211–215 and 229

Sana, 45, has run her family's business through two very stressful years. She now catches every cold that goes round, and her doctor has found that her blood pressure is raised. Using your knowledge of the autonomic nervous system, hormones and the immune system, explain how long-term stress could have affected Sana's health. 5 marks

Write your answer for 3 minutes. Then compare it with the model answer.

1

Describe (1 mark): long-term stress keeps Sana's body in a state of arousal, and this has weakened her defences and raised her blood pressure.

2

Name the systems (1 mark): her hypothalamus activates the sympathetic nervous system, and the adrenal glands release noradrenaline and cortisol.

3

Explain (2 marks): lymphocytes have receptors for these hormones, and cortisol reduces helper T cells, so she fights infection less well. Each stressful episode also raises her blood pressure, and with frequent stress the rise becomes permanent: hypertension.

4

Research (1 mark): Morrison and Bennett conclude that chronic stress "significantly impairs" the immune system (p. 215), and John Henryism links sustained effort against stressors to hypertension (p. 231).
