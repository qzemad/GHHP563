Section 1 of 5

Adherence

Taking medicines and following treatment advice as agreed with your doctor.

Why it matters: a medicine that isn't taken can't work.

Ten days of antibiotics. On day 4 you feel fine. What do you do?

Chapter 4 · pp. 88–90

Why? Your reasons:

This is not new: Hippocrates (about 400 BC) wrote that patients didn't take their medicines, and then complained that they didn't get better.

Compliance, adherence or concordance?

Chapter 4 · pp. 88–89

Doctors used to call patients who didn't take their medicine "non-compliant". What's wrong with that word?

Compliance

The patient obeys the doctor's orders. If they don't, they sound disobedient or careless. All the responsibility is on the patient.

Adherence

The patient sticks to advice they have worked out together with the doctor. The term health psychology prefers.

Concordance

Doctor and patient agree on the treatment after the patient has been told its costs and benefits. Describes the conversation, not the pill-taking.

WHO changed its definition too: from doing what "the clinical prescription" says, to following recommendations the patient has agreed to (WHO 2009).

How would you find out whether your grandmother really takes her blood-pressure tablets?

Ways to measure

Ask her · count the pills left · test her blood or urine · use a smart pill-bottle cap that records every opening. Every method has flaws: what people say and what the caps record only partly match.

The question changes the answer

"Do you take it exactly as prescribed?" 76% said yes. Asked in detail, 48% took less than prescribed and 52% missed doses (Rosser et al. 2011).

Two kinds of non-adherence: deciding, or forgetting?

Chapter 4 · pp. 90–91

Intentional

The person decides not to take it, or to take less. They have a reason.

"The tablets made me dizzy, so I stopped."

Unintentional

The person means to take it but doesn't: they forget, get confused, or can't.

"I was busy and it slipped my mind."

Sort these: click once for intentional, twice for unintentional. Then sort the reasons on our board.

Why it matters: the two have different causes, so they need different fixes. A reminder won't help someone who has decided to stop.

Why don't people adhere? Five types of reasons

Chapter 4 · pp. 90–91 · Sabaté (2003) · examples are local

What it meansExample

The patient

What the person knows, believes and feels: about the illness, the medicine, and their own ability to manage it.

"Medicines are chemicals; desi totkay are safer." "I'm too young for heart pills."

The illness

What the illness is like: symptoms, pain, and how serious it seems (not how serious it is).

High blood pressure has no symptoms, so it feels like nothing is wrong.

The treatment

The medicine itself: how many pills, how often, for how long, and its side effects.

Five tablets at four different times, one of which makes you drowsy at work.

Money and life

Social and economic factors: cost, education, distance to a pharmacy, living alone.

Buying this month's tablets means skipping something else.

The health system

How the doctor and clinic work: explaining the medicine, time with the patient, trust.

A two-minute OPD visit; nobody explained why the tablet matters.

Beyond Pakistan: in South Africa, hunger made HIV tablets harder to stomach, clinics were a long walk away, and some felt that taking pills meant doubting God's power to heal (Kagee and Deport 2010).

Exam-style question · 2.5 marksPractice for the short questions · case invented for class

Mr Qureshi, 58, has high blood pressure. He takes three tablets at three different times of day. He stops them when he feels well, and his son says he often "forgets".

Suggest two changes his clinic could make to improve his adherence, and explain why each would help.

Write your answer on paper. 5 minutes, on your own.

What a full answer includes

Make it simpler

Fewer doses a day, or one combined pill. Why: fewer, simpler doses are easier to remember and fit into the day.

Make it automatic

Link each dose to a routine ("after Fajr, with chai"); a pill box or phone alarm. Why: fixes forgetting (unintentional).

Photo: Stevepb, CC0

Make it make sense

Explain that high BP has no symptoms; ask about his worries; involve his son. Why: fixes deciding to stop (intentional).

Each change: name it, then explain why it works. Naming a change without a reason earns little.

Section 2 of 5

Healthy diet

Eating enough fruit and vegetables, and where our food habits come from.

Why it matters: low fruit and vegetable intake causes about 1.7 million deaths a year worldwide.

How much fruit and veg did you eat yesterday?

Chapter 4 · pp. 95–97

And how big is "one portion"?

0

is one portion: about one apple or banana. WHO advises 400 g a day, so "5 a day".

2 + 5

Australia asks for more: 2 fruit + 5 veg. Yet over 90% of Australian adults don't reach the vegetable target.

1 in 5

English children aged 5–15 eat 5 a day (2012). Among adults, about 3 in 10.

Is 5 enough? Across 450,000+ people, each extra portion lowered the risk of dying from heart disease, up to about 5 a day; after that, more made no extra difference (Wang et al. 2014).

Vegetarians get less cancer. So should we all go vegetarian?

Chapter 4 · pp. 95–96 · Huang et al. (2012)

The finding

A review of studies on 124,706 people: vegetarians had less cancer and fewer deaths from heart disease.

The catch

The vegetarians also smoked less and drank less alcohol, and many studies didn't allow for this. So we can't tell whether it was the diet or the other habits.

The question to ask of every study: "What else is different about these people?"

Overall, the evidence that fruit and vegetables protect is fairly consistent. Natural chemicals called antioxidants probably explain much of it, along with helping people stay a healthy weight.

What did your parents do to make you eat vegetables?

Chapter 4 · pp. 96–99

Learned at home

Children whose parents praised and explained ate more fruit and veg. Children who were warned or forced did not (Vereecken et al. 2010).

Taste wins

Asked why they choose their favourite food, children said "it tastes good" (67%) far more than "it's healthy" (22%) (Haste 2004).

Food Dudes

Children watched cartoon heroes who love fruit and veg, tasted them repeatedly, and won small prizes. They ate more, for months (Horne et al. 2009).

Knowing what's healthy isn't enough: just putting fruit on sale in schools changed nothing. And who cooks for a widowed grandfather? Only 13% of older men living alone ate 5 a day (Hughes et al. 2004).

Do vitamin pills protect you from heart disease or cancer?

Chapter 4 · pp. 99–101 · Issues box

The idea

Vitamins A, C and E and beta-carotene are antioxidants. People who get little of them from food have more heart disease and some cancers.

The test

When people were given vitamin pills or dummy pills at random: no clear benefit (12 trials, 104,196 people). Beta-carotene pills increased lung cancer in smokers.

The lesson

The positive claims came from weaker studies. Get vitamins from food, not pills, unless a doctor says you need them.

Who takes a multivitamin or a "taqat" tonic at home? What do they expect it to do?

How many minutes of exercise a week is enough?

Chapter 4 · pp. 99–102 · updated with WHO (2024)

0

Name every disease you think exercise helps prevent

Chapter 4 · pp. 102–105, 107

Harvard graduates

17,000 men followed for years. Those who walked or ran 30–35 miles a week were half as likely to die early as those doing 5 miles or less (Paffenbarger et al. 1986).

Walking at 61–81

Over 12 years, 22% of older men who walked over 2 miles a day died, compared with 43% of those who walked less (Hakim et al. 1998).

How it works

A stronger heart, better breathing, lower blood pressure, less body fat, stronger bones. Being fit protects you even if you're overweight.

Telomeres again: 10 men with early prostate cancer changed their diet, exercise, support and stress. After 5 years their telomeres were about 10% longer; in 25 men who didn't change, about 3% shorter (Ornish et al. 2013). A small, early study.

When do you feel good: during a hard workout, or after?

Chapter 4 · pp. 105–107

Exercise and depression

Taken together, 23 trials suggested exercise helps depression a lot. But in the 3 best-run trials the effect was much smaller, small enough that it could have been chance. Exercise worked no better than talking therapy (Mead et al. 2009).

The dip

During hard exercise, mood gets worse as it gets harder, and only improves once you stop. People who remember feeling bad may not come back (Hall et al. 2002).

Why it can lift mood

The body releases endorphins (natural painkillers) and less stress hormone; muscles relax. Exercise also distracts you and brings friends.

Too much is harmful too: some people become dependent on exercise. They feel guilty and irritable when they miss a session, and it can go with eating disorders. Moderation is healthier than extremes.

Why do people in Karachi exercise, or not?

Chapter 4 · p. 108

Reasons to exercise

Reasons not to

The book's reasons: to get fit, lose weight or change shape, stay healthy, feel better about yourself, reduce stress, see friends.

The book's barriers: no time, cost, no facilities, embarrassment, low confidence, no one to go with, and unsafe roads with no cycle paths.

Beliefs matter: adults aged 65–85 who saw ageing positively walked more, and kept walking more over time (Wurm et al. 2010). People who already exercise see more benefits and fewer barriers, so getting started is the hardest step.

Section 4 of 5

Screening

Testing people who feel healthy, to find a risk or a disease early.

Why it matters: finding cancer early saves lives, but tests also bring false alarms and worry.

Would you want to know?

Chapter 4 · pp. 108–110, 113

Test A

Tells you whether you'll develop an incurable brain disease in middle age.

Test B

Shows a high risk of breast cancer that an operation could prevent.

Test A is for Huntington's disease. Nothing can change the risk, so the result only helps people prepare for the future.

Test B looks for the BRCA1/BRCA2 genes. They greatly raise a woman's own risk, but cause only 4–5% of all breast cancers. Removing the breasts beforehand is an option.

Most people say they'd want a genetic test for cancer (60–80%), but fewer actually take one when it's offered (59% vs 66% who intended to) (Ropka et al. 2006).

Finding a risk, or finding a disease early?

Chapter 4 · pp. 108–111

Screening for a risk

Finds something that makes illness more likely, so you can act before it starts (primary prevention).

Screening for early disease

Finds an illness that has already started but has no symptoms yet, so treatment starts early (secondary prevention).

Sort: click once for a risk, twice for early disease.

Mammograms work

Fewer than 1 in 100 women screened has cancer, yet in Finland, women invited had 22% fewer breast-cancer deaths.

Smear tests work

Cervical cancer is the top cancer in UK women under 35, and is often fatal if untreated. Caught early, it can be treated.

The PSA test is weak

It misses about 15% of prostate cancers, and 2 in 3 men with a high result don't have cancer. No lives saved over 20 years.

The screening paradox: a good test, but most positives are false alarms

Chapter 4 · pp. 110–111 · updated with NHS breast screening figures (2024)

A woman's mammogram comes back "abnormal". Guess: what's the chance she actually has cancer?

1

Screen 100 women who feel healthy. Breast cancer is rare in this group: only about 1 of them has it.

2

The test finds her. That's 1 true positive.

3

But among the 99 healthy women, the test wrongly flags about 3. These are false positives.

4

So 4 women are called back, and only 1 has cancer: a 1 in 4 chance.

100 women screened (NHS figures)

96 normal result3 called back, no cancer1 cancer

Why: when a disease is rare among the people tested, even a small error rate among the many healthy people produces more false alarms than real cases.

Apart from money, what could screening cost a person?

Chapter 4 · pp. 111–114

Worry

Women feel anxious before a mammogram. Some who were called back and then told they were fine kept worrying, and some didn't come back next time (Montgomery and McCrone 2010).

Genetic results

Learning you carry a cancer gene is upsetting. For most, distress fades back to normal over time; some feel hopeless for years. Even a "good" result doesn't always reassure.

One-sided information

Screening leaflets and websites mostly list the benefits and rarely mention false alarms or overdiagnosis (Jørgensen and Gøtzsche 2004).

A screening programme is worth it only if: the disease is serious and common · there is an early stage to find · early treatment really helps · the test is safe, accurate and acceptable · there are enough machines, staff and treatment · the benefits are worth the cost · people get honest information to choose (Austoker 1994).

How would you get young men to check themselves?

Chapter 4 · pp. 115–116

Testicular cancer is the most common cancer in men aged 15–35. Caught early, 95–100% survive, but over half are found late.

Teaching isn't enough

In Shanghai, 266,064 women were either taught breast self-examination or not. Ten years later the death rate was exactly the same (0.10%). The taught group found more harmless lumps and had more biopsies (Thomas et al. 2002).

A simple plan works

Young men who wrote down when, where and how they would check themselves were far more likely to actually do it (Steadman and Quine 2004).

This is an implementation intention, an "if–then" plan: "If it's Friday after my shower, then I'll check." It works for pills too: "After Fajr, I take the blue tablet."

Section 5 of 5

Immunisation

Vaccinating people, mostly children, to protect them and the community from infectious disease.

Why it matters: Pakistan is one of only two countries where wild polio still spreads.

Why would a loving parent refuse a free vaccine?

Chapter 4 · pp. 116–120

How vaccines work

A harmless piece of the germ (an antigen) teaches the body to make antibodies. If 92–95% are vaccinated against measles, it can't spread: herd immunity.

Fear, not education

Parents who didn't vaccinate were more worried about the vaccine's risks and saw fewer benefits (Bennett and Smith 1992). People fear meningitis, but not measles or flu.

One bad study

In 1998 Wakefield wrongly linked the MMR vaccine to autism. UK uptake fell from about 97% to 81%. He was struck off as a doctor in 2010, but the fear lasted.

Measles is back

4,541 cases in early 2026, and 71 children died in four months. Only 84% of babies get the first measles dose, short of the 95% needed. 651,000 babies got no vaccines at all in 2025.

Sana, Karachi

A WhatsApp video said vaccines cause paralysis and infertility, so she refused. A health worker visited again and again for two months and answered her questions. She changed her mind, then her husband did.

What changed Sana's mind: the facts, or the relationship?

You're the health minister. Day 4, and only 16% of girls are vaccinated.

Chapter 4 · pp. 117, 119 · Pakistan campaign not in the book

September 2025: Pakistan's first HPV vaccine campaign, for 13 million girls aged 9–14. The HPV virus causes most cervical cancers; the vaccine prevents about 90% of them if given before infection. Videos online claim it causes infertility. In groups: what would you do?

The rumours

It causes infertility · "is it halal?" · "why only girls?" · "why is it free?" A viral video of crying schoolgirls was really tear gas footage from 2024.

What they did

Videos from religious scholars, doctors and celebrities · repeated home visits · women vaccinators on scooters · 3 extra days · the minister vaccinated his own daughter on live TV.

The result

About 9.2 million girls, roughly 72%. But 18 districts stayed below 60%. Before the campaign, only 2% of parents knew the vaccine existed.

Polio, measles, HPV: what made more people take the vaccine?

Chapter 4 · p. 120

Trusted messengers

Health workers, religious scholars, people like us.

Answer the real fear

Address the specific worry, such as infertility, not just "vaccines are safe".

Make it easy

Free, in schools and homes, with repeated visits.

Be honest

Give balanced, evidence-based information so people can make an informed choice.

The evidence is clear. Doing it is the hard part.

Chapter 4 · p. 120 · Summary

  • Adherence: about half of people don't take long-term medicines as prescribed. Deciding and forgetting need different fixes.
  • Diet and exercise: both protect health, but knowing isn't doing. Family, taste, environment and beliefs shape what we do.
  • Screening: it saves lives, but brings false alarms and worry. People need honest information to choose.
  • Immunisation: fear and trust, more than knowledge, decide whether parents vaccinate.
