Section 1 of 3 · Taylor Chapters 3 and 15

Health promotion

Health promotion helps people build good habits and drop bad ones before illness develops.

Why it matters: keeping Rashid Uncle healthy is far cheaper and kinder than treating his stroke.

Health promotion: an overview

Chapter 3 · Health promotion: an overview · p. 41 · Chapter 15 · Health promotion · pp. 326–328

Is your health your own job, your doctor's job, or the government's job?

Four benefits: fewer people die of lifestyle diseases, and people live longer. Most important, people get more years of life free from chronic disease. Health costs also fall.

Where it's heading (Ch 15): programmes should focus on morbidity (years lived with illness), not just deaths. They should tackle several habits at once, help people keep their changes, send "just-in-time" help by phone at the right moment, and link individual change to policy.

Your Greenwich Works project: how could you apply this? At which level is your project working?

Health habits and primary prevention

Chapter 3 · Health behaviors and health habits; primary prevention · pp. 41–42 · Belloc and Breslow (1972)

Score yourself: how many of these 7 habits do you have?

Do you sleep 7–8 hours, not smoke, eat breakfast every day, drink no more than 1–2 drinks a day, exercise regularly, avoid eating between meals, and stay no more than 10% overweight?

The Alameda County study

Researchers followed about 7,000 Californians. The more of the 7 habits people had, the fewer illnesses they had. 9–12 years later, men with all 7 habits had only 28% of the death rate of men with 0–3 habits. For women, the figure was 43%.

A health behaviour is something people do to improve or keep their health. A health habit is a health behaviour that is firmly set and done automatically, like brushing your teeth. Habits form in childhood and settle by about age 11–12, so they're hard to change.

Barriers to modifying poor health behaviours, and the teachable moment

Chapter 3 · Barriers to modifying poor health behaviors; using the teachable moment · pp. 43–44

Everyone knows smoking kills. Why do people still do it?

Barriers

  • No immediate payoff: bad habits do no visible harm for years, and few teenagers think about their health at 50.
  • Emotional factors: bad habits are pleasurable, automatic and often addictive, and scary messages can make people defensive.
  • Habits are unstable and unrelated: someone who exercises may still skip the seat belt, so habits must be tackled one at a time.

Teachable moments

These are times when people are ready to change, such as pregnancy, the weeks after giving birth, baby check-ups, the school physical, or a new diagnosis of heart disease.

Section 2 of 3 · Taylor Chapter 3

Social engineering and venues

We can change laws and surroundings, and choose where to reach people.

Why it matters: one tax can do more than a thousand leaflets.

Changing health behaviours through social engineering

Chapter 3 · Changing health behaviors through social engineering · p. 61

What cut Pakistani smoking more: warning pictures on the packet, or higher prices?

Social engineering changes the environment, often by law, so the healthy choice is easier or the unhealthy one harder. In the US, requiring vaccination for school entry means 90% of children are vaccinated, and a lower speed limit saved more lives than driver education. Other examples are a higher drinking age, workplace smoking bans, controls on school vending machines and sugary-drink taxes.

Pakistan, 2022–23 (Not in the book): cigarette taxes tripled, and the minimum price rose from Rs 63 to Rs 127. Production fell by more than 28% (WHO 2025).

The limit: most health behaviour can't be made law (you can't legislate breakfast), so psychology is still needed.

Changing the environment: nudges and cues

Chapter 3 · Social engineering · p. 61 · Supporting evidence: Morrison and Bennett, Chapter 7, pp. 188–189

Watch what people choose. Do they take the stairs or the escalator?

Play "Piano stairs" on YouTube ↗ It lasts about 2 minutes.

Is this social engineering? Would the effect last?

A nudge

Not every change needs a law. Making the healthy option easy, visible or fun can shift behaviour without banning anything.

Evidence

Simple "Take the stairs" posters at the foot of the stairs nearly doubled stair use in a shopping centre (Webb and Eves 2007).

Section 3 of 3 · Taylor Chapters 4 and 5

Public health in practice

Taylor applies prevention, social engineering and venues to four real problems: road accidents, obesity, body image and smoking.

Why it matters: the same tools work very differently for a seat belt, a burger and a vape.

Accident prevention

Chapter 4 · Accident prevention; motorcycle and automobile accidents · pp. 70–73

Hands up: did you wear a seat belt on the way here? Did anyone sit in the back without one? Was a phone used while driving?

Taylor: about 1.35 million people die on the roads each year (WHO 2018). Home accidents are the top killer of under-5s, so parents are taught to childproof. Older adults are most at risk from falls, and newer programmes target phones while driving. A seat-belt campaign worked a little and then slipped back, but laws work better: belt and helmet laws cut deaths.

Karachi's e-challan (Not in the book): cameras have fined drivers since 28 Oct 2025. On day one, the top offence was no seat belt, with 1,535 fines, and even the DIG Traffic's car was fined Rs 10,000. The front-seat passenger can be fined too. I found no rule for the back seat.

Obesity: taking a public health approach

Chapter 5 · Taking a public health approach · pp. 96–97 · Not in the book: Karachi, food prices

How many new cafés or restaurants have opened near you this year? How often does your family eat out?

Taylor: prevention starts with families at risk and with schools, which can serve healthy food and cut sitting time. The WHO proposed clearer labels, a "junk food tax" and limits on advertising to children. When US states limited junk food in schools, food companies fought "bitter battles" against them.

Karachi is eating out (Not in the book): dhabas and roadside stalls have given way to cafés, franchises and new food streets. Sugary drinks already carry a 20% excise duty; since June 2026 low-sugar drinks are exempt.

Can price change what we eat? Pooled studies show that when healthy food was 10% cheaper, people bought about 12% more of it. When unhealthy food was 10% dearer, they bought about 6% less (Afshin et al. 2017). Singapore pays restaurants up to S$40,000 to make and label healthier dishes, and over 300 brands have joined.

Body image and weight: Box 5.3 and plus-size models

Chapter 5 · Box 5.3 The Barbie beauty battle · p. 97 · Not in the book: recent research

Vote: do plus-size models help people feel better about their bodies, or do they make people less worried about their weight?

Box 5.3, the Barbie beauty battle: to match Barbie, a healthy young woman would need to be 2 feet taller with a waist 6 inches smaller. Ideals like this can't be reached, and they feed dieting and eating disorders. In 2016 Mattel added curvy Barbies.

Optional video · skip if short▶ Reverse Selfie · 1 min

Dove. Before: count how many edits she makes. After: does this help body image? Does it say anything about health?

The case for: average-size models sold products as well as thin ones, and women felt better about their bodies (Diedrichs and Lee 2011, 291 people). Body-positive Instagram posts raised young women's body satisfaction and mood (Cohen et al. 2019, 195 women).

The concern: among 23,459 overweight adults in England, more and more saw their weight as fine between 1997 and 2015, and those people were less likely to try to lose weight (Muttarak 2018). The study is correlational, so it shows a link but can't prove that plus-size images cause it.

Close: can a campaign reduce weight stigma and keep people aware of health risk?

Smoking prevention, and the rise of vaping

Chapter 5 · Public health approaches to smoking; smoking prevention programs · pp. 112, 115–116 · Not in the book: vaping in Pakistan

Hands up if you know someone at university who vapes. Do people see vaping as smoking?

What works against smoking: warning labels prompt quit attempts, but community campaigns are costly and their effects fade. Public bans and higher taxes "have been most successful" (p. 115). Free treatment in Massachusetts cut smoking among poor residents from 38% to 28%.

School prevention, the social influence approach: information is pitched at teenagers, the non-smoker is shown as independent, and peers are used to support not smoking. It can cut smoking for up to 4 years. Alcohol programmes use the same idea to teach drink-refusal skills.

Vaping (Not in the book): Taylor notes that "more youngsters now smoke e-cigarettes" (p. 112). In one survey, 68% of 389 Karachi university students said they vaped (Qureshi et al. 2024). A court blocked Punjab's 2025 ban, and a Senate bill (Oct 2025) would ban sales to under-18s, vaping in public and all vape adverts.
